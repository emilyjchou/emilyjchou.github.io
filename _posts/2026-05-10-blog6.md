# HCI Blog Series (6/6): A Quiet Reflection on HCI

This course didn’t feel dramatic to me.

There weren’t huge “aha” moments or sudden bursts of inspiration. Instead, it was a gradual shift in how I think—subtle, but consistent. Looking back, I realize that’s probably the point of HCI. It’s not loud. It doesn’t try to impress you. It just quietly changes how you see things.

Our project, **TripFlow**, focused on something simple: making trip planning less overwhelming. At first, it seemed like a straightforward problem—just organize information better, maybe add some helpful features.

But as we worked through the process, it became clear that the real challenge wasn’t the interface itself. It was understanding how people make decisions when they feel overloaded.

---

# Understanding the Problem

Trip planning involves a lot of small decisions:
- when to go  
- how much to spend  
- which flights to choose  
- where to stay  

Individually, these decisions aren’t that difficult. But together, they create friction.

What I learned early on is that good design doesn’t remove decisions—it makes them easier to navigate.

That idea shaped how we approached TripFlow.

---

# Iteration, Not Perfection

Our first prototype was simple, and honestly, incomplete.

During testing, users pointed out things we hadn’t considered:
- they couldn’t save options  
- they felt constrained by limited search choices  
- some actions weren’t as clear as we thought  

None of this was surprising, but it was still useful.

Instead of trying to fix everything at once, we made small, targeted improvements:
- clearer buttons  
- more flexible search options  
- better visibility of important information  

As we moved from low-fidelity to medium- and high-fidelity prototypes, the process felt less like “designing” and more like refining.

Each iteration wasn’t a big leap—it was just a step forward.

---

# What Changed in the Design

Most of our improvements came directly from user feedback:
- expanding cost calculations to include more realistic expenses  
- improving “vibe filters” so they felt less generic  
- adding more detailed hotel and location information  
- giving users more control to edit and adjust their plans  

One of the more important changes was allowing users to modify their trip after it was generated.

Initially, we focused on helping users create a plan. But we realized that users also want to adjust things—small changes, personal preferences, different priorities.

That shift—from generating solutions to supporting flexibility—was one of the more meaningful parts of the project.

---

# Using Heuristics in Practice

Nielsen’s usability heuristics were introduced earlier in the course, but they became more relevant toward the end.

When we applied them to our final design, they helped us notice smaller issues:
- whether users had enough control  
- whether the interface clearly communicated what was happening  
- whether actions felt predictable  

These weren’t major problems, but addressing them made the design feel more consistent and easier to use.

---

# What I Took Away From This Course

I wouldn’t say this course changed everything for me, but it did change how I approach design in a few important ways.

I think more about:
- clarity over complexity  
- reducing unnecessary effort for users  
- how small details affect usability  
- designing based on real feedback, not assumptions  

I also became more comfortable with iteration.

Not everything needs to be right the first time. In fact, it usually isn’t.

---

# Advice for Future Students

If you’re taking this course, here are a few things that might help:

**1. Don’t overthink your first design**  
It’s just a starting point. You’ll change it anyway.

**2. Pay attention during user testing**  
Even small observations can lead to meaningful improvements.

**3. Focus on usability, not just appearance**  
Something that looks good isn’t always easy to use.

**4. Take feedback as information, not criticism**  
It’s part of the process.

---

# Looking Forward

I’m not sure if I’ll directly work in HCI in the future, but I know the way I think about systems and interfaces has changed.

I notice design decisions more now—especially when something feels unclear or inefficient.

And when I build something, I think a bit more about how someone else might experience it.

---

# Final Thought

If I had to describe this course in one sentence, it would be:

> “A gradual shift toward thinking more carefully about how people interact with what we build.”

It wasn’t a dramatic experience, but it was a useful one.

And I think that’s enough.
